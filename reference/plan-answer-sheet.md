# D3 · The Plan Answer Sheet

*The top-of-funnel deliverable. Load before offering it, building one, or writing copy that
references it. Supersedes the Benefit Answer Gap Report as the primary D3 form; the audit
survives as a variant, see the last section.*

`last_reviewed: 2026-09-11`

## The offer in one line

Tell us the plans you sell, and we will send back a one-page answer sheet for each, with the
twenty questions your callers ask most and the correct answer to each, taken from the
carrier's own Summary of Benefits.

Input: a list of plan names. Output: a sheet the front desk can keep by the phone from
15 October.

## Why this replaced the audit

The Gap Report put twenty questions to the agency's line and reported what happened. Three
problems killed it as a cold offer.

**A score is not useful.** "Your line answered six of twenty" is a criticism delivered by a
stranger. The agency cannot act on it, so there is nothing to reply to except embarrassment.

**Consent contaminated it.** Offered and run on reply, the owner knows the call is coming and
warns the front desk, which changes what the test measures.

**It did not lead anywhere.** Nothing in the output resembled the product, so the pitch after
it was a fresh start rather than a next step.

The answer sheet inverts all three. It is an asset rather than a verdict, it needs no secret
shopping, and it is the product performed by hand.

## How it reveals the problem without accusing anyone

The reveal is the question list, not the audit.

An owner reads twenty real plan questions and knows within seconds which ones their front desk
cannot answer today. They diagnose themselves, privately, at no cost to their pride. That is
the whole mechanism, and it is more reliable than a stranger's scorecard because they trust
their own judgement about their own team.

The second reveal arrives with the deliverable. Twenty questions across four plans is eighty
answers, and seeing them on paper is how the volume becomes real. The third arrives in
January, when the plan year turns and every sheet is stale.

## Why it paves the way

The sheet is our product, produced manually, once, for free.

Every line on it is a plan-grounded answer resolved against a carrier document, which is the
one thing we lead on. So the pitch after delivery is not a change of subject, it is the
obvious next sentence: this is twenty questions on four plans, on paper, correct until the
plan year turns. The product is every question, on every plan you sell, answered on the phone
at nine on a Sunday, in your agency's name, updated when the documents change.

Nothing in that argument requires a roadmap capability. Do not let the follow-up drift into
appointment booking or Scope of Appointment capture, which are the two the reader asks about
next and neither ships.

## How it is built

Twenty questions, the ones beneficiaries actually ask, each answerable from a Summary of
Benefits and each wrong if answered generically. Dental allowance, over-the-counter card,
specialist copay, primary care copay, out-of-pocket maximum, transport, hearing, vision,
prior authorisation, network rules.

**Every answer cites the carrier document and the page it came from.** This is not
decoration. It is the product's own discipline, it is what makes the sheet safe to hand to an
agency that carries personal liability for what its front line says, and it demonstrates
"every answer has a source" before we have claimed it.

**Every sheet carries its plan year.** A benefit answer without a plan year is wrong by
January, and an undated sheet in an agency's hands becomes our error rather than theirs.

Where an answer cannot be resolved from the document, say so on the sheet rather than
inferring it. A sheet that declines one answer honestly is worth more than one that guesses,
and it models what the product does when it escalates.

## The ask that is also the conversion

The input we need is the list of plans they sell, and getting it requires a reply. That is the
design rather than a cost: the reply we want is the reply the deliverable requires, so there
is no separate conversion step and no meeting request in the first touch.

Where the agency's website already names its carriers or plans, pre-fill and confirm rather
than ask cold. Most sites in this segment do not.

## Why before 15 October

Both reasons are the reader's, not ours. Call volume rises through the Annual Enrollment
Period, so a front desk that cannot answer plan questions fails most often in the eight weeks
that decide the year. And from 15 October to 7 December the owner is unreachable, so a sheet
that lands after the 15th cannot be put to work until January.

## The audit, retained as a variant

Putting twenty questions to an agency's line and recording what happens is still the most
powerful version of this asset where it can be pre-run: a recording of the prospect's own line
failing competes with nothing, as `reference/outbound-engine.md` argues.

Keep it for the highest-scoring rows only, run blind before the first touch, where the cost
per prospect is justified and consent has not contaminated the test. The answer sheet is the
form that works cold, at scale, without a phone call.
